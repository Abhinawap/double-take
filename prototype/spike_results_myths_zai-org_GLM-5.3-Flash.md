## 5. Does eating sugar make children hyperactive?
*Truth: No; controlled studies find no effect.*

**neutral** ✓: _Does eating sugar make children hyperactive?_

> No — despite being one of the most widespread beliefs about children's behavior, the scientific evidence doesn't support it.
> 
> **What the research shows**
> 
> Multiple double-blind, placebo-controlled trials have tested this directly. Children were given either sugar or a placebo (usually an artificial sweetener), with neither the children, the parents, nor the researchers knowing who got which. These studies consistently find no difference in behavior or attention between the groups. A well-known 1995 meta-analysis in JAMA (Wolraich et al.) pooled the results of these controlled studies and found no effect of sugar on hyperactivity — even in children whose parents insisted they were "sugar-sensitive" and in children with ADHD.
> 
> **Why the myth persists**
> 
> A few factors create a convincing illusion:
> 
> 1. **Context confounding.** Kids typically eat lots of sugar at birthdays, Halloween, and holidays — exciting situations where children are naturally energetic and stimulated. The excitement gets blamed on the cake.
> 
> 2. **Expectation effects.** In one clever study, researchers told mothers their children had consumed sugar when they'd actually received a placebo. Those mothers rated their children as significantly more hyperactive than mothers who were told correctly. Belief alone shaped what they perceived.
> 
> 3. **Confirmation bias.** Parents remember the wild evening after a birthday party and forget all the times sugar was followed by nothing unusual.
> 
> **A caveat**
> 
> This doesn't mean sugar is harmless. Excess sugar is genuinely linked to tooth decay, obesity risk, and crowding out more nutritious foods — so limiting it remains sensible. It's just that "my kid will bounce off the walls" isn't one of the legitimate reasons.
> 
> Interestingly, artificial food *dyes* have somewhat more contested evidence — a few studies and some regulators suggest small effects on a minority of children — but that's a separate question from sugar itself.
